User guide

How it works

Every view, every layer, every key

Open Overwatch is a live view of the planet built only from public data: satellites, aircraft, ships, earthquakes, storms, weather, cameras and more, on a 3D globe that keeps zooming out through the Solar System to the cosmic web. There is also a classic 2D map. No account, no key, nothing to install.

This page explains what each part shows, where its data comes from and how to drive it: with a mouse, a finger, the keyboard or your voice.

The views

3D globe

globe.html, built on CesiumJS. The Earth with real day and night, clouds and city lights, every satellite CelesTrak publishes at its real height, aircraft, ships, storms and many more layers. Fly from orbit down to a street. It is the main view and most of this guide is about it.

Solar System

solar.html, built on three.js. The planets and 20 major moons at their real positions, 1.57 million asteroids, comets, spacecraft, nearby stars, 6,339 exoplanets, the Milky Way and 43,480 galaxies. Zoom out far enough on the globe and it takes over by itself.

2D map

open-overwatch.html, built on Leaflet. The original situation map: aircraft, satellites, earthquakes, disaster alerts, news, radar, cables, cameras and ships on a flat map, with a daily brief, a feed log and a setup tab for optional keys.

Tonight

tonight.html. The globe's "Tonight above you" card on its own page, without the 3D globe, so it opens fast on a phone: what you can see in your sky tonight and when.

The 3D globe

Moving around

Mouse and touch

  • Drag to turn the globe, scroll or pinch to zoom, right-drag (or two fingers) to tilt.
  • Click anything (a dot, a marker, a country) for its card. Touch picks within a wider area than the mouse, so small dots are easy to tap.
  • Esc stops following an object, or closes the card when nothing is followed.

The navigation pad (desktops)

Bottom right: + and − zoom, the compass needle points north and snaps the view north-up and level (N), ⤓ looks straight down (T), ⎘ copies a link to this view, </> copies embed code for your own web page, ◫ shares a picture (P) and ◎ resets to the whole Earth (R). North-up and top-down ask you to stop following first.

The phone dock

On phones the long band and the time buttons are replaced by a dock at the bottom: Explore (every destination and tool in the band, as a sheet), Layers, Time (the clock speeds), Look up and North. The buttons click the same controls as on a desktop, so everything works the same way.

The time bar

Top of the screen. « runs backwards an hour a second, ❚❚ pauses, ▶ is live, › is a minute a second, » ten minutes a second, ⏩ an hour a second, and NOW returns to the present. Satellites, the Sun, the Moon and the weather follow the clock. Aircraft hide while the clock is away from live, because there is no record of where they were. Go further back with Time travel: earthquakes, storms, natural events and satellites then switch to their archives. The clock carries over into the Solar System view and back.

The layers panel

The Layers button (top right) opens the dock of switches listed in Globe layers, plus:

  • Base map: Satellite imagery (Esri World Imagery) or Dark map (Esri dark grey canvas). Far out, NASA's Blue Marble and, on the night side, Black Marble city lights are drawn over it.
  • Graphics: Auto, High, Balanced or Low. Auto starts from your device and steps down if frames get slow. Lower levels draw fewer aircraft and satellite models and coarser tiles.
  • Battery saver: fewer frames (5 to 15 a second) while nothing moves; full speed returns on any touch, key or motion.
  • Under the hood (also the ` key): frame rate and times, draw calls, the GPU, every data source and how old its data is, and the satellite worker's timing.

Your layer choices are remembered in this browser.

Click anything

Cards, following and the cockpit

  • Satellites: what the satellite is in one sentence, its orbit, height and speed, whether it is in sunlight, and When can I see it? (its passes over you in the next 3 days). Space stations say how many people are aboard.
  • Aircraft: what it is, what it is doing and where it is going (see Planes and airports), and Share (a link that finds that flight wherever it is when opened).
  • A country: click the ground and its border lights up, with facts (Wikidata, World Bank), what the globe knows about it right now (earthquakes, lighthouses, big employers, the wind) and English-language headlines from its news outlets (GDELT).
  • Everything else (quakes, storms, fires, ships, buses, cameras, stations, volcanoes, launches, drawings) opens its own card.

How we judge this: where each live item came from

Every live card (aircraft, ships, satellites, quakes, storms, fires, events, transit, cameras, bikes, buoys, tides, air quality and the rest, on the globe and on the 2D map) ends with a line saying which sources reported it, how many of them agree, how old the data is and a confidence word. A contact on the map is a claim by its sources, not proof: two networks placing the same aircraft in the same place, or NOAA and GDACS placing the same storm, count as agreement; a feed that only repeats another agency's data (EONET passing on an NHC advisory) is named as such and not counted twice. Hover the confidence word for the rule:

Confirmed: two or more independent sources agree and their data is fresh. Reported: one fresh source. Stale: the newest data is older than this layer expects. Unknown: no source said when its data was made. Age is the age of the data, not of the download.

"(copy)" after a source means it reached us through a copy: the website's own mirror (satellite element sets, launches), the public relay, or a stored snapshot. Its age is still the age of the data itself. Each layer has its own idea of fresh: two minutes for an aircraft position, fifteen for a ship, nine hours for a storm advisory, three days for a satellite's elements. Time travel shows past records, which are never called stale.

Export what's in view: the 2D map's layer list and the globe's Layers dock save the live items in view as GeoJSON, CSV or KML, each with these provenance columns. Nothing leaves your browser. The MCP server's answers carry the same line as a provenance field.

Follow

Double-click a satellite or aircraft (or press Follow in 3D on its card) and the camera rides along with it while you orbit around it. Esc lets go.

Cockpit

While following, press C (or CKPT in the Ops rail) to ride the satellite (or, in the download version, the plane) looking along its path. Drag to look around, the wheel or a pinch zooms the lens, the arrow keys turn your head, Esc gets out. The model and its orbit line hide while you are inside.

Aviation

Planes and airports

Live aircraft need the download version: the ADS-B networks refuse websites. There, military aircraft refresh every 60 seconds and a plane you watch every 10 seconds; everything in this section works with the planes the globe has.

The aircraft card

  • What it is: its class (airliner, heavy, prop, turboprop, helicopter, fighter: the 3D model follows it), maker and type, registration, owner, the country its address belongs to, and a photo, from adsbdb (asked only for the plane you look at; answers kept in your browser for a day).
  • What it is doing: its flight phase in a pilot's words (taxiing, take-off, climbing, cruise, descending, approach, final, landing roll) and its height above the nearest field; "On final for 34R" when it is lined up with a runway.
  • Where it is going: the route its callsign usually flies, shown only when it fits where the plane actually is, as a dashed arc to the destination; the airports at either end open their boards.
  • Its trail: where it has been in the last half hour, coloured by altitude, for the plane you follow or open. Pin keeps a trail drawn after the card closes; Copy GeoJSON takes it with times and altitudes. Trails start when a plane is first heard here.
  • Tell me when it lands: a notification when it is on final and when it touches down (keep the tab open).
  • From the tower: watch it from the airport's tower with a lens that keeps it framed; Esc or Leave the tower gives the camera back.

Airport boards

Click an airport (the airports layer) for its board: the planes arriving (low and descending towards it), departing (climbing away), on the ground, and those that just landed or took off, worked out from the live aircraft around it, with emergency squawks standing out. It is not a timetable: a plane counts once it is close and low enough to tell. The card also has the weather at the field with its VFR or IFR category, the runway in use and the wind on each runway, Follow the next arrival and From the tower.

Arrival times are estimates with a range, such as ≈ 12 min (9–17): worked out from how far the plane is, how fast it is going and how fast it is coming down. The range is narrow once it is lined up with a runway and wide when it is far out or still has to turn round, because approaches add holds and vectors no feed can see. Departures say about how long ago they took off.

In the download version the card also shows:

  • The airport's METAR and TAF (from the Aviation Weather Center) in plain words: wind, visibility, weather, cloud, temperature and pressure now, and the forecast period by period with its flight category, plus the reports exactly as sent. Airport dots in view are coloured by flight category: green VFR, blue MVFR, red IFR, magenta LIFR. The website shows the model weather instead, because aviationweather.gov does not answer websites.
  • Runways used, last hour: while the card is open (or after Keep watching, up to three airports), planes near the field are asked for a fresh position every 15 seconds, and each landing and take-off is matched to its runway. The card counts them per runway and says when the airport changed direction. Each landing also scores the arrival estimates made for it, and the card says how they have been doing at that airport.

Cockpit weather

Riding a plane in the cockpit (or with the HUD on while following one), the HUD shows its flight phase and the weather where it is: the wind at its height and whether it is a head or tail wind, the outside temperature against the standard atmosphere, true airspeed and Mach, cloud at its level, an icing hint, and the nearest airport and destination. From Open-Meteo, asked only when the plane moves to another cell or level.

Re-entries

Falling soon

Falling soon in the band (or a globe.html#go=reentry link) lists the objects predicted to come back into the atmosphere in the next 30 days: old Starlinks, spent satellites, debris. Each says when, as a window ("between Tue 03:00 and Thu 21:00 UTC") rather than a moment, and where, as a corridor: the stretch of its ground track it could come down on. The reentry layer draws every corridor at once. Nothing is downloaded for this: the globe works it out from the element sets it already has, in about a tenth of a second.

How it is worked out

Each element set says how fast the object's orbit is shrinking (the change in its mean motion, n-dot) and how much drag it feels (B*). From those and a model of the upper atmosphere, the globe flies the object down, step by step, until its lowest point is 100 km up: below that it is minutes from the end. The atmosphere is thicker when the Sun is active, so the model is scaled by the Sun's radio flux (F10.7; 140 is assumed for late 2026). Checked against real re-entries: Tiangong-1 (2 April 2018) and GOCE (11 November 2013) fall inside the windows their last element sets give.

Why the windows are so wide

Re-entry predictions are uncertain by nature, even at the space agencies. The window is ±25% of the time the object has left, the usual rule of thumb for objects coming down without control (re-entry analysts quote 10-25%; we take the wide end):

  • The air up there changes. At 200-400 km it is so thin that one solar flare or geomagnetic storm can double its density within a day, and nobody can forecast the Sun weeks ahead.
  • We don't know how the object flies. A tumbling panel has several times the drag of the same panel edge-on.
  • The elements are a few hours old and are themselves a fit to radar tracking. Every new set (every few hours) moves the window, and it narrows as the day comes closer: weeks ahead it spans days, the last day it spans hours.
  • Where follows from when. An object circles the Earth every 90 minutes, so an hour's doubt in time is 40,000 km along its track. Until the window is a few orbits long, it can come down anywhere between the latitudes its orbit reaches; the tracks drawn are the orbits around the likeliest time.

Satellites that still have fuel are a different case: an operator can keep one up, or bring it down on purpose over an empty ocean, and then the prediction does not apply. Their cards say so. Objects whose orbit is clearly being pushed (shrinking faster than drag could manage, or growing) are left off the list. The card's provenance line uses the element set's epoch as its data time and calls a prediction stale once its elements are older than a fifth of the time it had left. Most of what re-enters burns up; for authoritative warnings, follow your space agency.

Stargazing

Tonight and look up

Tonight above you

The Tonight button (or tonight.html, or globe.html#go=tonight) asks for your location once and then says, in plain words:

  • Visible passes of the ISS, Tiangong and Hubble, Starlink trains still low after launch, and the brightest satellites, each marked clear or cloudy from the cloud forecast (Open-Meteo, location rounded to about 10 km).
  • The five bright planets, the Moon's phase, moonrise and moonset, sunset, darkness and sunrise, the next full Moon by its name (and whether it is a supermoon).
  • Meteor showers with an honest rate from where you are, the chance of aurora where you stand, the next solar eclipse you can see from home, meetings of the Moon and planets in the next 30 days, launches within 1,000 km in the next week, and the spacecraft farthest from home.
  • Calendar downloads an .ics file with a 10-minute reminder that works with the site closed; Remind me shows a notification while the page is open. Share tonight sends a one-line summary with a link.

Sections fold and stay folded. Your location is saved on your device only, rounded.

Look up

Your sky from where you stand. On a phone, hold it up and the view follows the phone's motion sensors; on a computer, drag. It shows planets, bright stars, constellation figures (Figures), satellites and aircraft overhead, a compass ring and the next ISS pass. Things on the ground are hidden so they don't streak across the sky.

  • Find: pick a planet, the Moon, the ISS or a bright star, and an arrow leads you to it ("turn right 32°, up 11°").
  • Tap anything in the sky to learn what it is: distance, light travel time, "the light reaching your eyes left Vega in 2001".
  • Sky on: any date from 1800 to 2049 (planets, Moon and stars; satellites only within 3 days of today). Now returns to the present.
  • Red: a red-light view that keeps your eyes used to the dark (remembered). Done or Esc leaves.
Weather mode

The weather timeline

Weather in the band opens one timeline that runs from what satellites and radar saw in the last couple of hours into what a weather model forecasts for the next five days (three on phones). Nothing is downloaded until you open it.

Pick the forecast model in the panel: NOAA GFS (1° grid, the default) or ECMWF IFS (0.25°, ECMWF's open data, CC BY 4.0), or compare the two side by side with a slider between them. Both are read straight from their public files in your browser, a few fields at a time. A legend says what the colours mean, and pointing at the globe reads every field at that spot.

Clouds, rain and lightning start on. Drag the slider, or press play: the globe's clock moves with it, so the day-night line and the satellites move with the weather, and moving the clock in the time bar moves the slider. Closing weather mode puts the clock back to live.

Time travel

Time travel

Time travel in the band (on phones: Time in the dock) opens a panel on the globe's one clock. Drag the slider (the last 30 days; around the day shown when you go further back), type a date and time (UTC), step an hour or a day, or jump 1 day, 1 week, 30 days or 1 year back. The clock stops at the moment you pick; press ▶ or a speed in the time bar to let it run from there, and Now to come back.

On this day in history takes you to a moment, switches on the layers that tell its story and flies there: the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami (2011), Hurricane Ian's landfall in Florida (2022), Typhoon Haiyan (2013), the Türkiye–Syria earthquakes (2023), the James Webb Space Telescope's launch (2021) and Apollo 11 (1969).

What has history, and what doesn't (the panel says the same, and what came back for the time shown):

  • Earthquakes: the USGS catalogue, back to 1900. The 24 hours before the time shown, magnitude 2.5 and up (at most 1,500 a day, the biggest first); each appears as the clock passes it.
  • Tropical cyclones: NASA EONET's past storms and GDACS's past cyclones, roughly since 2000: where each storm was at the time shown and its track until then. Forecast cones and warnings of the time are not archived, so none are drawn.
  • Natural events (the events layer): EONET's events going on that day, including ones closed since.
  • Satellites: the website keeps a copy of the element sets every day for 30 days (space stations, bright, navigation, weather, geostationary, science and the debris clouds). Each satellite uses the set nearest the time shown; one with nothing within 7 days is hidden rather than drawn in the wrong place. Starlink, OneWeb and "all other active" use today's sets within a week of today and hide further back.
  • Live only: aircraft and upcoming rocket launches (hidden in the past), cameras, ships, transit, bikes, traffic, weather, aurora, the ocean and air-quality layers and the VIIRS fire hot spots (they keep showing now).

Copy link in the panel copies the view with its time (t=) and the panel open (go=history).

Measure

Draw and measure

Measure in the band, M, or your voice. Pick a tool and click (or tap) on the globe:

  • Line: each leg and the total length. On 3D terrain, finished lines up to 30 km also get the length over the hills and the climb and descent.
  • Area: the area and perimeter of the shape you draw.
  • Distance: an arrow from one place to another, with the bearing, and optionally the trip by road (OSRM).
  • Pin: a named marker.
  • Outline a country: its border and its area.

Distances are measured on the real shape of the Earth (the WGS84 ellipsoid, within a millimetre of the reference library GeographicLib) and areas on it too. Up to 512 points. A quick second click on the last point, Enter or Finish ends a shape; Backspace, Delete or Ctrl+Z undoes the last point; Clear removes the drawing; Copy GeoJSON takes it to other mapping software. Share links carry the drawing (see Share and embed).

On phones, while a tool is armed the card shrinks to a slim strip above the dock with the running total, Undo, Finish and a chevron for the rest.

Share, embed and pictures

A link to this view

The ⎘ button (phone menu: Share) copies a link that puts the next person exactly where you are: the camera, which layers are on, the time if you moved the clock, the weather sources if weather mode is open, the country card, the flight you are looking for, the Ops look, and your drawing. Everything is in the part of the address after #; nothing is stored on a server. A very large drawing is left out of the link (Measure → Copy GeoJSON keeps it).

Links that open a panel by themselves work too, for example globe.html#go=tonight or globe.html#go=launches. The 2D map's Share button writes the same format, so a link opens the same area in either view. The full format, for anyone building links (the Claude extension does), is in docs/LINK_FORMAT.md.

Put the globe on your web page

The </> button copies an <iframe> of the current view in embed mode: just the globe, no toasts about location or reminders, no Ops rail, and an "Open Overwatch ↗" link that opens the full site at the same view. Paste it into any page.

Share a picture

◫ or P takes the view with a caption strip and the site's address. On phones it goes to the share sheet with the link; on computers you get a PNG and the link is copied.

Install as an app

Your browser can install the site as an app (Add to Home Screen on phones). It opens full screen, with shortcuts to Tonight and Find a flight. If your device asks for reduced motion, camera flights jump instead of swooping.

The classic view

Show

  • Moons: 20 major moons from Mars to Pluto, on orbits fitted to JPL Horizons.
  • Dwarf planets, Asteroids (1.57 million from JPL's Small-Body Database, each class switchable: main belt, near-Earth, Jupiter Trojans, Centaurs, Kuiper belt and beyond, Mars-crossers, other) and Comets.

  • Eclipses & occultations: the shadows during the solar and lunar eclipses of 2026 to 2030 and the Moon passing in front of bright stars and planets (see below).
  • Stars near the Sun (HYG catalogue) and Our radio bubble: how far our broadcasts have travelled since 1920, with the stars inside tinted.
  • Planets of other stars: 6,339 exoplanets from the NASA Exoplanet Archive at their stars; fly into a system to see real sizes and the habitable zone.
  • Light delay: a ghost of the focused body where Earth sees it, because its light is minutes or hours old.
  • Milky Way & galaxies: our galaxy seen from outside and 43,480 galaxies of the 2MASS Redshift Survey, with the big clusters named.

Eclipses and occultations, from space

Eclipses & occultations 2026-2030 (left panel) lists every solar and lunar eclipse and every time the Moon covers Aldebaran, Regulus, Spica, Antares, the Pleiades or one of the five bright planets, with where on Earth it is seen. Jump sets the clock to the greatest moment and turns the view to look at it; ▶ plays it from the start at ten minutes a second. During a solar eclipse the Moon's shadow is a dark spot crossing the globe, outlined, with its shadow cones reaching back to the Moon; during a lunar eclipse the Moon turns red inside rings that mark Earth's umbra and penumbra; for an occultation a tube shows the Moon's "shadow" from the star sweeping over Earth. Each event also has a card (search "eclipse 2027" or "Regulus") whose timeline holds its contacts. The Sun and Moon come from NASA JPL Horizons; the times agree with NASA's eclipse canon to within two minutes.

Where is it now?

The search box finds planets, moons, dwarf planets, spacecraft, every comet, about 26,000 named asteroids, nearby stars and other planetary systems, by name, nickname or a near-miss spelling ("Halley", "Juno", "Eros"), and flies there.

Wow

Guided tour: Earth to Andromeda, Send a light pulse from the Sun (a shell of light spreads out at 60 times its real speed and names each planet it reaches), Next planetary alignment, and Under the hood: speed, and the positions' error measured live against NASA JPL Horizons. Three lists: Deep-space missions now (every mission with its distance and signal time to Earth, nearest first, and what it does next), Comets: brightest now (the brightest comets with their predicted magnitude and when each peaks, the ones due to peak brighter than magnitude 12 in the next three years, then the best placed ones; magnitudes from JPL's comet parameters, and comets often beat or miss them) and Eclipses & occultations 2026-2030. The time bar runs from a year a second backwards to a year a second forwards; planet positions are good from 1800 to 2050.

On your machine

The download version

The website works with nothing installed. The download adds what a website can't reach: live aircraft and military flights. The ADS-B networks and OpenSky send no permission for web pages to read them and refuse cloud servers, so a small helper on your own computer asks them for you.

  1. Get open-overwatch.zip from the latest release (about 70 MB) and unzip it into one folder.
  2. On Windows, double-click Start Open Overwatch.bat. On Mac or Linux, run python3 serve.py or node serve.js in that folder. It needs Python 3 or Node 18 or newer.
  3. Your browser opens the map at http://127.0.0.1:8787/open-overwatch.html; the globe is at /globe.html.

The helper serves the folder on your computer only and relays a fixed list of sources, only for its own pages. It has no dependencies and talks to nothing else. To update, download the new zip.

On your phone or desktop

App, offline and accessibility

  • Install it. Where the browser allows (Chrome, Edge, Android) an Install button appears; on an iPhone use Safari's Share menu → Add to Home Screen. It opens full screen with shortcuts to Tonight, Find a flight and the 2D map.
  • Offline. Every page and its code is kept once visited, so the app opens with no connection. A banner says when you are offline and how old the saved data is; live feeds are never passed off as fresh. Map tiles you have seen are kept for 30 days.
  • Updates. When a new version is published the page offers a reload; nothing changes under you mid-visit.
  • Long press (phones) on the globe or the 2D map opens that spot: its coordinates (decimal, degrees-minutes-seconds, MGRS), Copy, a link that opens the globe there with a pin, and on the globe the country and a pin in your drawing. A first-visit card shows the touch gestures once (?hints=1 shows it again).
  • Keyboard and screen readers. Every page has a skip link and focus rings for the keyboard; toolbars move with the arrow keys; the globe turns, zooms and tilts from the keyboard (see Keyboard); a selected satellite, country, planet or plane is announced once. If your device asks for reduced motion, animations stop and camera flights jump.

The download version leaves the offline copy off unless you open it with ?sw=1 (and ?sw=0 removes it), so several local copies don't get mixed up.

MCP

Ask Claude about the Earth

Open Overwatch also comes as a small Model Context Protocol server, so Claude (and other AI apps that speak MCP) can answer questions from the same open data, with 37 tools: earthquakes, where a satellite is and when you can see it, launches, who is in space, asteroid flybys, close approaches in orbit and what is about to re-enter, tropical storms, wildfires, natural events, volcanoes, Baltic ships, bike share, live transit, traffic cameras (with their latest pictures), measured road speeds, submarine cables, country briefings, the weather (with pressure, gusts, waves and whether the models agree), what a plane is and where a flight goes, where any spacecraft, comet or asteroid is, the aurora and the solar wind, flares and storm warnings behind it, tonight's sky, the Sun and Moon, the planets, meteor showers, eclipses, distances, licence-plate readers, military areas, airport weather (METAR and TAF in plain words), sea conditions (ocean buoys and tides) and air quality. Every answer carries a link that opens the globe there, and in Claude Desktop the globe appears in the chat. Install it from open-overwatch.mcpb on the latest release; details in mcp/README.md. Your data

Privacy

  • No accounts, ads, cookies or analytics. Your settings (layers, keys, folded sections, a rounded location for Tonight) stay in your browser's own storage.
  • Your browser talks to the data providers directly to load the map and the data, and to a code CDN (jsDelivr, cdnjs). The fonts are served from this site. Those services can see your IP address under their own policies, and the map area you look at is requested from them.
  • Your location (Tonight, Look up, Around me) is used on your device. The cloud forecast for Tonight sends it to Open-Meteo rounded to about 10 km.
  • Searches and spoken places send only the words to Photon (komoot). Directions go to the FOSSGIS routing servers; mapped cameras to the public Overpass API; buildings, military areas and plate readers come as map tiles from OpenFreeMap and FlockHopper.
  • Aircraft details (type, owner, photo, route) are asked of adsbdb for the plane you open, and cockpit and airport weather of Open-Meteo for the plane's or the field's position. The offline copy lives in your browser's storage only.
  • Voice uses your browser's speech recognition: Chrome and Edge send the audio to Google or Microsoft, Safari to Apple. This site receives none of it, and the microphone listens for one command each time you press the mic or V, or while you hold Space.
  • The public relay on Cloudflare carries a few feeds a browser can't read directly (NOAA hurricanes, NYC cameras, some camera lists and pictures, cable routes). It carries only those and runs no logging code of its own.
  • Weather forecasts come straight from NOAA's and ECMWF's public files on Amazon Web Services; a spoken weather question sends the place it is about to Open-Meteo.
  • Photoreal 3D, if you turn it on, uses your own Cesium ion token and sends it only to Cesium. Radio reports each station you start to radio-browser.info.

The full notice, with every service named, is on the landing page.

Read this

Limits

Open Overwatch is a hobby project for curiosity and awareness. Never use it for navigation, flying, sailing, driving, emergencies or anything where a late, wrong or missing position could hurt someone. Everything comes from third parties and can be late, incomplete or wrong; positions broadcast by the transmitter itself (aircraft, ships, balloons) can be spoofed.

  • Satellite positions come from public element sets that are hours old; the error grows by roughly 1 to 3 km a day.
  • Aircraft on the website are only what it can reach (none of the live ADS-B networks); the download version has them.
  • Cars in the traffic layer are simulated; only the measured roads in New York and Finland are real readings, and New York's are often a day late.
  • Infrared clouds don't cover Europe, Africa or the Indian Ocean; the forecast does.
  • Launch replays are reconstructed estimates, not telemetry.
